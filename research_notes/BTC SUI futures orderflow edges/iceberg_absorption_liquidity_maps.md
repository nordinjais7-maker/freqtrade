Most findings therefore come from search-engine extracts of the cited pages rather than full-text reads. Treat exact quotes as "per search extract" and re-verify before publishing. ## Q1. Iceberg orders: definition, detection from trades + L2, academic evidence, crypto applicability

### Takeaway
The equity and futures literature (pre-2018, used here for mechanism only) shows that hidden and iceberg liquidity is large, partly predictable and has price effects. Reliable detection, however, needs order-ID (market-by-order, MBO) data or exchange-native iceberg flags. Binance, Bybit and OKX perpetuals publish aggregated price-level (MBP/L2) books, and I found no evidence that they offer native iceberg orders. On these venues "icebergs" are heuristically inferred refill patterns at a price level. They cannot be told apart from synthetic icebergs (algos re-posting), market makers re-quoting, or independent traders joining the level. I found no peer-reviewed study of iceberg detection or iceberg-signal profitability on crypto perps.

### Cited Findings
- [ACADEMIC, equities, mechanism] Bessembinder, Panayides & Venkataraman (2009, JFE), Euronext-Paris: hidden orders were 44% of sample order volume. Hidden orders had lower implementation-shortfall costs but were less likely to fill completely and took longer to execute. The presence and size of hidden orders "can be predicted to a significant, but imperfect, degree" from observable order attributes. — [SSRN 956476](https://papers.ssrn.com/ab_id=956476)
- [ACADEMIC, equities] Hautsch & Huang (CFS WP 2012/04), NASDAQ TotalView-ITCH: where fully hidden liquidity sits is predictable from the observable market state. Traders weigh competing for hidden liquidity against protecting themselves from adverse selection, front-running and HFT "hidden order detection strategies". — [CFS working paper](https://gfk-cfs.de/media//12_04.pdf); related Hautsch/Huang work at [HU Berlin edoc "On the Dark Side of the Market"](https://edoc.hu-berlin.de/18452/5041)
- [ACADEMIC, Xetra] Frey & Sandås ("The Impact of Hidden Liquidity in Limit Order Books"; CFR WP 09-06): iceberg orders on Xetra averaged 12–20x the size of ordinary limit orders. A detection algorithm was used, and the predicted presence of icebergs was associated with larger incoming market orders. They also study how undetected icebergs affect mid-quote moves. — [CFR Cologne WP 09-06](https://www.cfr-cologne.de/download/workingpaper/cfr-09-06.pdf); [AoF conference draft 2008](https://www.aof.org.hk/uploads/conference_detail/516/con_paper_0_505_session-6-3_freysandasiceberg_7sep08.pdf)
- [ACADEMIC, CME futures] Zotikov & Antonov (2019), "CME Iceberg Order Detection and Prediction": detects and predicts native and synthetic icebergs on CME, with out-of-sample evaluation. Builds on Christensen & Woodmansey (2013) and notes that having order IDs "drastically changes the logic of the detection step". — [arXiv 1909.09495](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.09495)
- [ACADEMIC] Dall'Amico et al. (2019): estimate latent liquidity. Synthetic icebergs are spotted by limit orders arriving within a short window after a trade at the same price. — listed in [NEP-MST 2019-09-30](https://nep.repec.org/nep-mst/2019-09-30)
- [ACADEMIC, survey] Treatment of the hidden quantity "var[ies] greatly from one exchange to another". A synthetic iceberg looks the same as a trader who keeps re-submitting a limit order at the same price and size. — [Gould et al., Limit Order Books, arXiv 1012.0349](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1012.0349)
- [ACADEMIC, India] HFTs make extensive use of hidden limit orders, and hidden orders lose time priority. — [NYU Stern / NSE white paper](https://stern.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/assets/documents/2017%20White%20Papers%20%281%29.pdf)
- [VENDOR, data vendor] dxFeed: synthetic icebergs are processed by the exchange like any other order and "can only be detected heuristically", relying on a set of assumptions. Native icebergs can be detected unambiguously when the feed has order IDs. — [dxFeed Iceberg Detection](https://dxfeed.com/solutions/iceberg-detection-solution/)
- [VENDOR] Bookmap: "A snapshot or instant state of the market depth doesn't allow detecting Iceberg orders regardless of market data quality". Detection needs event dynamics, mainly new liquidity appearing immediately after executions at a price level. The "native" algorithm uses MBO. The "Resistance" algorithm approximates it from lower-quality data and is said to be nearly as accurate only if MBO-to-MBP conversion keeps the exact event sequence. Bookmap also warns that older marker logic is no longer reliable because event sequencing in feeds has changed. It states "almost all Digital/Crypto exchanges provide MBO data", but this is unverified for Binance/Bybit perps (see Inferences). — [Bookmap wiki: Iceberg Orders Tracker](https://bookmap.com/wiki/Iceberg_Orders_Tracker)
- [INDUSTRY] BitMEX supports hidden and iceberg orders, with a fee schedule for them. This shows native icebergs exist on at least one crypto derivatives venue. — [BitMEX support](https://support.bitmex.com/hc/en-gb/articles/6909929283997-What-Fees-Do-Hidden-and-Iceberg-Orders-Pay-on-BitMEX)
- [INDUSTRY] Binance.US lists iceberg as an order type but states "Iceberg orders are not supported" (spot venue, not USDⓈ-M). I could not confirm USDⓈ-M futures iceberg support from Binance's official futures API docs. Binance has moved some conditional order types off `/fapi/v1/order` to an "Algo Service", per a [binxr changelog](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/binxr/news/news.html). — [Binance.US support](https://support.binance.us/en/articles/9842889-conditional-order-types-post-only-iceberg-time-in-force-what-they-are-how-to-use-them)
- [ANECDOTAL/educational] Generic glossaries claim that most major CEXs (Kraken, Binance, Bitfinex, OKX) support iceberg or hidden-quantity functionality. They do not separate spot from perps or exchange-native from client-side algos. — [Spark glossary](https://www.spark.money/glossary/iceberg-order)

### Inferences
- On Binance/Bybit/OKX perps, any "iceberg" signal is a refill heuristic: repeated trades at price P exceed the displayed size at P, and the displayed size reloads within ms after each fill. Inference from the Bookmap and dxFeed descriptions and from these venues offering aggregated depth streams: Binance `depth@100ms`/diff-depth is per-price aggregated, so individual orders cannot be tracked. The same pattern comes from (a) an exchange-side or broker iceberg, (b) a client algo slicing an order, (c) several market makers re-quoting, and (d) unrelated traders joining. Labels therefore cannot be validated without order-ID ground truth, which is not public for these venues.
- 100 ms book-diff throttling on Binance perps means fills and refills within one update window can merge. This makes refill detection noisier than on CME MBO. (This is an inference from the update cadence. I did not verify it against current docs in this session.)
- The equity results (hidden size predicts larger incoming market orders, partial predictability) are about execution and liquidity. They do not show that "trade in the iceberg's direction" earns returns after costs. No source found makes that claim with out-of-sample evidence.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed crypto study of iceberg prevalence or predictive power was found, on spot or perps.
- I could not verify from official docs whether Binance USDⓈ-M, Bybit or OKX perps offer exchange-native iceberg order types; arxiv and exchange doc fetches were blocked. OKX offers an "iceberg" algo order on its trading interface (not verified in this session).
- No published accuracy figures for Bookmap's or Exocharts' iceberg detectors on crypto feeds.

## Q2. Absorption: definition and formal tests as a reversal signal

### Takeaway
"Absorption" is a practitioner term: heavy aggressive (market-order) volume at a level with little or no price progress, read as passive limit liquidity soaking up the flow and a likely reversal. I found no academic paper that formally tests absorption as a reversal signal in crypto or elsewhere. The only "tests" found are TradingView scripts that report in-sample hit rates on the viewer's chart.

### Cited Findings
- [ANECDOTAL/educational] Absorption = heavy aggressive volume at a price that does not break through. The source states there is "no reliable published win rate for absorption entries". — [TheRightTrader](https://therighttrader.com/learn/volume-and-order-flow/absorption)
- [VENDOR, LuxAlgo] Absorption "prints large volume at a level that refuses to break", while exhaustion prints dwindling volume on the final push. "Neither guarantees a turn." — [LuxAlgo concept page](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/absorption-and-exhaustion/)
- [ANECDOTAL, open-source script] "CVD Absorption Outcome Statistics" reports how often each absorption type was followed by a reversal on the current chart, with sample size and a minimum-event threshold. This is in-sample and chart-specific. — [TradingView script](https://kr.tradingview.com/script/UgaF1Yk8-CVD-Absorption-Outcome-Statistics-Dots3Red/)
- [ANECDOTAL, open-source script] An order-flow profile script forward-tests its reversal signals and reports base rates with Wilson intervals "to distinguish a real edge from a small-sample illusion". It also warns that without L2 data "every delta here is a proxy" and that bar-level delta cannot locate volume inside the bar. — [TradingView script](https://br.tradingview.com/script/PF7kVFtK-Order-Flow-Profile-Microstructure-Calibrated-Sweeps/)

### Inferences
- Absorption has a theoretical link to the Q1 literature, since refilling passive liquidity (iceberg or re-quoting) is the mechanism. Mechanism is not edge, though. Both "absorption then reversal" and "absorption then breakout once the passive seller is exhausted" are narrated after the fact, which makes the concept vulnerable to hindsight labelling.
- The concept is backtestable from public data. Binance/Bybit/OKX aggTrades/trades carry an aggressor side, and archived trade data is publicly downloadable for Binance. Define the event as signed volume over N seconds ≥ k × rolling median, with |price change| ≤ m × ATR or tick-bounded, then measure the forward-return distribution against matched non-event controls. The book-side part ("passive size held") needs historical L2, which requires a paid archive (e.g. Tardis-style providers). That is an inference, not verified here.

### Gaps
- No academic or rigorous practitioner study (out-of-sample, after fees and slippage) of absorption on crypto perps was found.
- No vendor (Bookmap, Exocharts, TradingLite) publishes hit rates for its absorption markers.

## Q3. Spoofing and layering in crypto order books: prevalence and implications

### Takeaway
Spoofing in crypto books is well documented anecdotally and studied with detection methods, but no peer-reviewed prevalence rate exists for Binance/Bybit/OKX perps. High cancellation rates are normal everywhere, so cancellation alone is not evidence. The practical implication is still strong: large resting "walls" far from the touch are frequently pulled, so displayed-depth signals (book imbalance, "liquidity maps" built from L2 heatmaps) carry an unknown share of non-bona-fide liquidity. The share is likely highest away from the touch, exactly where liquidity-hunting narratives place their levels.

### Cited Findings
- [ACADEMIC] Fabre & Challet (2025), "Learning the Spoofability of Limit Order Books with Interpretable Probabilistic Neural Networks": real-time spoofing detection on centralized crypto exchanges. Uses multi-scale Hawkes-process order-flow features and estimates a spoofer's expected gain. Key result: models that ignore the posting distance of new limit orders from the best prices are inadequate. — [arXiv 2504.15908](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15908v1)
- [ACADEMIC] "Detecting Financial Market Manipulation with Statistical Physics Tools" (2023) uses L3 (order-level) data for LUNA/USD and BTC/USD. A standard Z-score approach missed inserted synthetic spoofs and falsely flagged large legitimate orders. Order size alone is not a reliable spoof marker. — [arXiv 2308.08683](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.08683)
- [INDUSTRY, secondary summary of the above] Spoof orders tend to sit in passive areas of the book to create directional pressure while avoiding fills. L3 data is costly and hard for the public to get. — [Cointelegraph Research](https://cointelegraph.com/research/crypto-market-spoofing-identifying-fake-orders-and-their-impact)
- [ACADEMIC] A GRU-based spoof detector for retail protection, with a 2020 Kraken ETH/USD event as the example. — [arXiv 2110.03687](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03687)
- [ACADEMIC, agent-based] About 95% of NASDAQ limit orders are cancelled (cited estimate), so cancellation rates alone do not prove spoofing. The paper models when spoofing is profitable against agents that use book imbalance. — [Wang & Wellman, Games 2021, "Spoofing the Limit Order Book: A Strategic Agent-Based Analysis"](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4336/12/2/46)
- [INDUSTRY, journalism] April 2025: a roughly 2,500 BTC (about $212M) sell order on Binance that looked like resistance vanished abruptly. CoinDesk discussed whether spoofing was "back". This is one incident, and intent was not established. — [CoinDesk 2025-04-29](https://www.coindesk.com/business/2025/04/29/a-vanishing-usd212m-bitcoin-order-caused-chaos-for-traders-is-spoofing-back-in-crypto)
- [INDUSTRY, secondhand via Bloomberg] Solidus Labs flagged over $170M of unusual orders by two Hyperliquid accounts in oil perps, with a cancel-to-execution ratio of about 30:1. Intent unproven. — [Wu Blockchain on X citing Bloomberg](https://x.com/WuBlockchain/status/2115574724038451222)
- [ANECDOTAL, vendor-affiliated blog, no methodology] Claims that about half of large BTC walls near price are pulled rather than traded, and that walls more than $75 from price were cancelled 92% of the time. Unverified. — [DEV Community post](https://dev.to/stangenberg295spec/bitcoin-order-book-what-our-own-data-showed-us-and-what-it-didnt-5b3e)
- [INDUSTRY, regulators] CFTC spoofing enforcement (e.g. the January 2018 anti-spoofing sweep) has targeted traditional futures (metals, energy, etc.). My searches found no CFTC spoofing action against trading on offshore crypto perp venues, which are largely outside CFTC surveillance. — [CFTC enforcement actions index](https://www.cftc.gov/LawRegulation/EnforcementActions/index.htm?page=68); [Katten "Bridging the Week"](https://katten.com/bridging-the-week-featuring-articles-on-spoofing-related-sanctions-and-a-bitcoin-etf-denial)

### Inferences
- If spoofers profit specifically against imbalance-following agents (Wang & Wellman's mechanism) and posting distance is the key spoof feature (Fabre & Challet), then a naive imbalance or wall signal using deep-book levels is the most exploitable kind. Imbalance restricted to the top few levels, weighted toward executed rather than displayed liquidity, is more robust in principle.
- L2 "liquidity heatmaps" (Bookmap, TradingLite, Exocharts) show displayed size over time. They cannot separate bona fide from spoof liquidity, because MBP feeds do not show order identity or lifetime per order. A level's "persistence" in the heatmap partly helps, but a spoofer can keep an order resting until price approaches.
- On thin altcoin perps such as SUI, a given notional wall is a much larger share of depth than on BTC, so manipulation is cheaper and the fake-liquidity share is plausibly higher. This is inference, with no SUI-specific study found.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed estimate of spoof or fake-liquidity share on Binance/Bybit/OKX perps (BTC or alts).
- Exchange self-surveillance statistics for these venues are not public.

## Q4. Liquidation heatmaps / liquidity maps: how estimated, vendor disclosures, independent evaluation of "magnet" effect

### Takeaway
Coinglass, Hyblock and similar heatmaps are model estimates. They take open-interest changes and price, assume entry prices plus a fixed set of leverage tiers (typically 25x/50x/100x), and project where those hypothetical positions would be liquidated. They are not observed orders or positions. The vendors acknowledge this: Coinglass calls the output a "relative intensity" whose actual liquidations are "often lower", and Hyblock says its heatmap shows levels "opening but not closing". No vendor publishes accuracy or calibration statistics. I found no peer-reviewed test of the "price is magnetised to liquidation clusters" claim. The only test found is an unfinished personal GitHub project.

### Cited Findings
- [VENDOR, Coinglass] The heatmap "predicts where liquidations might be triggered", not the actual number of liquidations or exact closing prices. Intensity is relative, and "the actual liquidations is often lower than the intensity indicated by the heatmap". Data is aggregated from Binance, OKX, Bybit, BitMEX and others, with timeframes from 12h to 1y. The exact formula, leverage weights and binning are not published. — [Coinglass Learn: How to use Liquidation Heatmaps](https://www.coinglass.com/learn/how-to-use-liqmap-to-assist-trading-en) (content via search extract; direct fetch blocked)
- [VENDOR, Hyblock] Levels are "based on clusters of price points where highly leveraged traders open long or short positions. High leverage is identified as 100x, 50x, and 25x." The heatmap "calculates the liquidation levels based on market data and different leverage amounts". Calculated levels are added to price buckets, and the heatmap shows levels "opening but not closing". The LiqLevels indicator says predictions rest on leverage, position size, entry price and margin requirements. Hyblock says a dense cluster "may indicate that price might move toward that region". — [Hyblock Academy: Liquidation Levels](https://academy.hyblockcapital.com/tools/liquidation-levels); [Liquidation Heatmap](https://academy.hyblockcapital.com/tools/liquidation-levels-1); [LiqLevels](https://academy.hyblockcapital.com/indicators/liquidity-indicators/liqlevels)
- [ANECDOTAL, third-party review] The Hyblock API output is "every open position Hyblock can infer, bucketed by leverage": positions are inferred, not observed. Coinglass and Hyblock both use 25/50/100x tiers, and CoinAnk colours 25–40x up to 80–100x. Fixed tiers are an assumption, since real leverage use varies. — [Coinmonks/Medium, "Not Every Heatmap Is the Same"](https://medium.com/coinmonks/not-every-heatmap-is-the-same-ebb9251b43c6)
- [VENDOR, Kingfisher] Markets "LiqMaps" and claims it "pioneered forecasting of market liquidations". Self-reported user satisfaction claims (e.g. "85.8% report better outcomes") come with no published methodology. — [Product Hunt listing](https://www.producthunt.com/products/the-kingfisher/reviews); [Alchemy dapp listing](https://www.alchemy.com/dapps/thekingfisher)
- [ANECDOTAL, TradingView] Community clones of the Coinglass map state they differ from the real map, which underlines that the method is proprietary. — [TradingView open-source script](https://fr.tradingview.com/script/2WzPEzZ4/)
- [ANECDOTAL, personal project] The only explicit magnet test found: a GitHub PR tests whether the heavier side of a liquidation band is touched first within 4h. Calibration showed a planted magnet scored 60–69% "heavier side first" against 43–55% on random seeds. That noise band is wide, so a weak real effect would be hard to detect. No result on real data was visible. — [dejixy/analytix PR #86](https://github.com/dejixy/analytix/pull/86)
- [ACADEMIC preprint] A study of seven BTC perp liquidation cascades (2022–2025) on early-warning signals reports, among other things, that "price shows no early warning". It does not test cluster magnetism. — [arXiv 2607.27070](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27070v1)
- [ACADEMIC, DeFi] OECD WP No. 48: DeFi liquidations have a positive, significant effect on DEX price volatility. This is evidence that liquidations amplify moves, not that clusters attract price. — [OECD 2023](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2023/07/defi-liquidations_89cba79d/0524faaf-en.pdf)
- [ACADEMIC/working paper] An analysis of the Oct 10–11, 2025 cascade (GARCH/DCC-GARCH) concludes that reflexivity drives instability. It is not a magnet test. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/396645981_Anatomy_of_the_Oct_10-11_2025_Crypto_Liquidation_Cascade_Macroeconomic_Triggers_Market_Microstructure_and_Systemic_Risk_Lessons)
- [INDUSTRY, journalism relaying vendor charts] Typical media framing: "Bitcoin liquidity magnets determine BTC's directional price moves", based on vendor heatmap screenshots. — [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/markets/bitcoin-liquidity-clusters-determine-btcs-price-direction-as-futures-flow-fuels-price)
- [INDUSTRY, exchange docs] Ground-truth liquidation data is itself degraded. Binance's USDⓈ-M and COIN-M liquidation streams (`<symbol>@forceOrder`, `!forceOrder@arr`) push only the latest liquidation per symbol in each 1000 ms window, as a snapshot. In bursts, the observed liquidation tape undercounts. — [Binance Liquidation Order Streams](https://developers.binance.com/docs/derivatives/usds-margined-futures/websocket-market-streams/Liquidation-Order-Streams); [All Market Liquidation Order Streams](https://developers.binance.com/docs/derivatives/usds-margined-futures/websocket-market-streams/All-Market-Liquidation-Order-Streams)

### Inferences
- Structural reasons a "magnet" effect would look real even if it is not: (1) clusters are computed at fixed leverage distances from recent prices (about 1%, 2% and 4% away for 100x/50x/25x before fees and maintenance margin), so they sit at roughly constant distances above and below price. Ordinary volatility touches the near ones often regardless of causality. (2) The map shows levels "opening but not closing", so stale clusters accumulate and are later "hit". (3) Charts shared after the fact show touched clusters, not untouched ones. The right null is "probability of touching any level at distance d within horizon h", estimated from returns alone. A magnet claim must beat that base rate, and it must beat it on the heavier side versus the lighter side, as in the GitHub test design.
- A plausible genuine mechanism exists: forced liquidations are market orders, so when price reaches a dense real cluster, cascades extend the move (consistent with the OECD volatility finding). This supports "momentum or overshoot through a cluster once reached" more than "price is attracted to the cluster beforehand". These are different trading rules.
- Historical reconstruction for a backtest: vendor heatmaps are proprietary, and point-in-time history is generally available only through paid API tiers (Hyblock API, Coinglass API). Revisions and look-ahead in vendor history cannot be ruled out without vendor documentation. A transparent clone can be rebuilt from public data: OI history (Binance futures OI statistics at 5m granularity; raw OI deltas), price/VWAP of the bars where OI rose, assumed leverage tiers and maintenance margin. Validation against the realized liquidation tape is limited by the 1000 ms snapshot throttle on Binance. Bybit and OKX provide their own liquidation feeds, whose completeness I did not verify.

### Gaps
- No vendor (Coinglass, Hyblock, Kingfisher, CoinAnk) publishes accuracy, calibration or out-of-sample hit rates for its liquidation level estimates.
- No peer-reviewed study of cluster magnetism. The GitHub test has no visible real-data result.
- Exact Coinglass formula not disclosed. Direct fetch of the methodology page was blocked, so only vendor learn-page extracts were used.

## Q5. Rigorous practitioner backtests after costs; common biases; vendor-marketing share

### Takeaway
I found no rigorous, independently replicated backtest showing that iceberg detection, absorption, spoof-aware book signals or liquidation-heatmap "hunting" make money after fees and slippage on Binance/OKX/Bybit perps. Most available material is vendor education or marketing (Coinglass, Hyblock, Kingfisher, Bookmap, LuxAlgo) or ICT/SMC-style discretionary content using annotated screenshots. The academic literature supports the mechanisms (hidden liquidity exists and is partly predictable; spoofing exists; liquidations amplify volatility). It does not support trading rules built on them in crypto perps.

### Cited Findings
- Absorption: "no reliable published win rate" (educator, [TheRightTrader](https://therighttrader.com/learn/volume-and-order-flow/absorption)). Vendor LuxAlgo states the signals do not guarantee a turn ([LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/absorption-and-exhaustion/)).
- Liquidation magnets: the only testing framework found is a personal GitHub project with no visible real-data result ([dejixy/analytix PR #86](https://github.com/dejixy/analytix/pull/86)). Vendor claims are framed as "may indicate" ([Hyblock Academy](https://academy.hyblockcapital.com/tools/liquidation-levels)).
- Iceberg: the out-of-sample detection study is on CME with MBO data and concerns detection, not trading P&L ([Zotikov & Antonov 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.09495)). Vendors concede that synthetic icebergs are heuristic-only ([dxFeed](https://dxfeed.com/solutions/iceberg-detection-solution/)).
- Spoofing: academic work focuses on detection, not on a fade-the-spoof strategy, and needs L3 data that is "costly and hard for the public to obtain" ([Cointelegraph Research summary](https://cointelegraph.com/research/crypto-market-spoofing-identifying-fake-orders-and-their-impact); [arXiv 2308.08683](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.08683)).
- Good practice exists in a few open scripts: base rates with Wilson intervals and minimum sample sizes ([TradingView order-flow profile script](https://br.tradingview.com/script/PF7kVFtK-Order-Flow-Profile-Microstructure-Calibrated-Sweeps/)).

### Inferences
- Conflicts of interest: Coinglass, Hyblock, Kingfisher, Bookmap, Exocharts and TradingLite sell subscriptions or data whose value depends on the signal being perceived as useful. ICT/SMC educators sell courses. No vendor in this set publishes out-of-sample performance. Rough estimate from this search: more than 80% of easily findable material on these topics is vendor, educator or media content relaying vendor charts. This share is an impression from the search results, not a measured figure.
- Common biases to expect and test for:
  - Cherry-picked annotated charts, where clusters or icebergs that "worked" are shown.
  - Hindsight labelling: the same event is called absorption before a reversal and "exhaustion of passive seller" before a breakout.
  - Non-point-in-time vendor data, where the heatmap is recomputed after the fact.
  - Ignoring base rates of touching nearby levels.
  - In-sample only on one chart or symbol.
  - Survivorship in pair selection: the alts traded today are those still listed.
  - Ignoring fees, funding, slippage and latency. Order-flow signals act on seconds-to-minutes horizons where taker fees (about 4–5 bp per side on Binance USDⓈ-M base tier, not re-verified here) can exceed the gross edge.
  - Multiple-testing from tuning thresholds.
- Backtest feasibility summary:
  - Absorption from trades: feasible from free public trade archives.
  - Iceberg/refill heuristics: need tick-level L2 history (paid archives) and cannot be ground-truthed.
  - Spoof-aware imbalance: needs L2 (L3 is not public for these venues), so only proxies such as wall lifetime and cancellation-near-touch.
  - Liquidation heatmaps: rebuild a transparent clone from OI and price, or buy vendor history whose point-in-time integrity is unverified. Validation is weakened by throttled liquidation feeds.

### Gaps
- No independent, cost-inclusive, out-of-sample practitioner backtest was found for any of the four concepts on crypto perps. The absence may partly reflect search limits (about 17 calls; blocked direct fetches of arXiv and vendor pages).
- SUI-specific evidence of any kind: none found.
